## What Is the Height (Altitude) of a Right Triangle?

The altitude (also called the height) of a right triangle is the perpendicular segment drawn from the vertex of the right angle to the hypotenuse. It is usually labeled with the letter **h**. This segment meets the hypotenuse at exactly 90 degrees.

### What Makes It Special
The altitude does something remarkable: it divides the original right triangle into two smaller right triangles, and all three triangles are similar to each other. This similarity is the reason the projection formulas work.

### The Two Projections
Where the altitude meets the hypotenuse, it splits the hypotenuse **c** into two segments called projections, labeled **p** and **q**. By convention, **p** is the projection adjacent to leg **b**, and **q** is the projection adjacent to leg **a**. Together, p + q = c.

### Comparison Table: Height vs Legs

| Segment | Location | Defining Trait | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| Altitude | From right angle to hypotenuse | Perpendicular to hypotenuse | h |
| Leg a | Forms the right angle | One side of the 90° corner | a |
| Leg b | Forms the right angle | One side of the 90° corner | b |
| Projection p | Part of hypotenuse | Adjacent to leg b | p |
| Projection q | Part of hypotenuse | Adjacent to leg a | q |

> **Quick Tip:** The altitude is always strictly shorter than either leg. If your answer for h is longer than a leg, something went wrong.

## Why the Altitude Matters

The altitude is not just a classroom exercise. It connects the sides, angles, area, and projections of a right triangle through a single value, which makes it a powerful checking tool.

### It Links Everything Together
If you know the altitude and one other measurement, you can often recover the entire triangle. Engineers use this property when only partial measurements are available on site.

### It Verifies Your Work
Because h can be computed several different ways, calculating it twice by two methods is one of the fastest ways to confirm a triangle solution is correct. If h = ab/c and h = √(pq) give the same number, your triangle is consistent.

### It Appears in Area Problems
The area of any triangle is (base × height) / 2. For a right triangle, using the hypotenuse as the base and the altitude as the height gives Area = (c × h) / 2, which rearranges into one of the altitude formulas.

> **Formula Box:** h < a and h < b (always true for the altitude to the hypotenuse).

## Altitude Formulas Explained

There are four main altitude formulas. Each one starts from different known values.

### Meaning of Each Variable
- **a, b:** the two legs forming the right angle.
- **c:** the hypotenuse.
- **h:** the altitude from the right angle to the hypotenuse.
- **p, q:** the projections of the legs onto the hypotenuse (p + q = c).
- **A, B:** the two acute angles.

### Formula Summary Table

| Known Values | Formula to Find Height |
|---|---|
| Legs a and b, hypotenuse c | h = (a × b) / c |
| Projections p and q | h = √(p × q) |
| Area and hypotenuse c | h = (2 × Area) / c |
| Leg b and angle A | h = b × sin(A) |
| Hypotenuse c and angle A | h = c × sin(A) × cos(A) |

### When to Use Each One
Use the legs formula when you know all three sides. Use the projection formula when working with segments of the hypotenuse. Use the area form when area is already known. Use the angle forms when trigonometry is in play.

### When NOT to Use Them
Do not mix projections from different triangles, and do not use these formulas on non-right triangles. The relation h² = p × q holds only when h is the altitude to the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

## Methods to Calculate the Height

There are five practical methods, depending on what information you start with.

### Method 1: From the Legs
**When to use it:** You know legs a and b (and can find c).
**How it works:** First find the hypotenuse with c = √(a² + b²) (see the [Pythagorean theorem formula guide](/blog/pythagorean-theorem-formula/) for details), then apply h = (a × b) / c.
**Step-by-step example:**
Given a = 3, b = 4.
1) c = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.
2) h = (3 × 4) / 5 = 12 / 5 = 2.4.

### Method 2: From the Projections
**When to use it:** You know the projections p and q of the legs onto the hypotenuse.
**How it works:** The altitude is the geometric mean of the projections: h = √(p × q).
**Step-by-step example:**
Given p = 4, q = 9.
1) p × q = 36.
2) h = √36 = 6.

### Method 3: From the Area
**When to use it:** You know the area and the hypotenuse.
**How it works:** Since Area = (c × h) / 2, rearrange to h = (2 × Area) / c.
**Step-by-step example:**
Given Area = 30, c = 13.
1) 2 × 30 = 60.
2) h = 60 / 13 ≈ 4.615.

### Method 4: Using Angles
**When to use it:** You know one acute angle and one side.
**How it works:** h = b × sin(A), or equivalently h = c × sin(A) × cos(A).
**Step-by-step example:**
Given b = 10, A = 30°.
1) sin(30°) = 0.5.
2) h = 10 × 0.5 = 5.

### Method 5: Special Right Triangles
**When to use it:** Your triangle is exactly 30-60-90 or 45-45-90.
**How it works:** Use the fixed side ratios, then apply h = (a × b) / c.
- 30-60-90 with short leg s: legs are s and s√3, hypotenuse 2s.
- 45-45-90 with leg s: hypotenuse is s√2, and h = s / √2 = s√2 / 2.

## Real-World Applications

The altitude shows up wherever perpendicular measurements matter.

### Construction and Carpentry
When framing a roof, the altitude from the ridge to the ceiling joist tells the builder the exact vertical depth of the attic space. Carpenters compute it from the rafter triangle legs rather than measuring in place.

### Engineering and Surveying
Surveyors use the projection relationship when they can measure along the ground (the hypotenuse) but cannot reach the raised point directly. The altitude gives the vertical component from horizontal distance segments.

### Navigation and Aviation
When an aircraft descends along a glide path, the altitude of the descent triangle relates the along-track distance to the height lost. Controllers use the same right-triangle geometry.

## Common Mistakes

Avoid these mistakes when finding the height of a right triangle:

1. **Using a leg as the altitude:** The legs are heights too, but the altitude formulas here give the height to the hypotenuse, which is a different, shorter segment.
2. **Forgetting p + q = c:** The projections must add up to the hypotenuse. If they do not, the inputs are inconsistent.
3. **Applying h² = pq to non-right triangles:** The geometric mean relationship holds only for the altitude to the hypotenuse of a right triangle.
4. **Mixing up p and q:** Swapping them does not change h (multiplication commutes), but it scrambles the leg relationships a² = cq and b² = cp.
5. **Skipping the hypotenuse:** In h = ab/c, students sometimes divide by a leg instead of the hypotenuse.
6. **Calculator in radians mode:** When using sin or cos with degrees, the calculator must be in degree mode.
7. **Rounding too early:** Keep full precision through intermediate steps and round only the final answer.
8. **Expecting h longer than a leg:** The altitude to the hypotenuse is always shorter than either leg. A longer result means an error.
