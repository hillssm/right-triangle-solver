An **isosceles right triangle** is the right triangle with two equal sides: the two legs that form the right angle are the same length, its angles are **90°, 45°, and 45°**, and its hypotenuse is always the leg length multiplied by √2. That single ratio makes it the easiest right triangle to solve. If one leg is 5, the other leg is 5, the hypotenuse is 5√2 ≈ 7.07, and you are done: one known side fixes everything. This article covers why the equal sides must be the legs, the formulas for sides, area, and perimeter, and worked examples in both directions.

## What Is an Isosceles Right Triangle?

It is a right triangle in which the two legs are equal. Three facts follow immediately:

- **The equal sides are the legs**, the sides that meet at the right angle. The hypotenuse can never be one of the equal pair, because the hypotenuse is strictly the longest side. If it equaled a leg, the third side would have to satisfy a² + a² = a², which is impossible for a real triangle.
- **The angles are 90°, 45°, and 45°.** Equal sides face equal angles, and the two acute angles must sum to 90°, so each one is exactly half of that. Our [angles in a right triangle](/blog/angles-in-a-right-triangle/) guide shows the complementary rule behind this.
- **It is exactly half of a square.** Cut any square along a diagonal and you get two isosceles right triangles. This picture explains most of the triangle's behavior, including its one line of symmetry, which runs from the right-angle corner to the middle of the hypotenuse.

The shape also goes by its angle name, the **45-45-90 triangle**. The two names describe the same triangle from different directions, one by its sides and one by its angles.

## The Side Ratio: 1 to 1 to √2

Every isosceles right triangle, whatever its size, has sides in the same proportion:

**leg : leg : hypotenuse = 1 : 1 : √2**

The ratio comes straight from the Pythagorean theorem. Call each leg s. Then c² = s² + s² = 2s², so c = s√2. Since √2 ≈ 1.4142, the hypotenuse is always about 41 percent longer than a leg.

| Leg s | Hypotenuse s√2 |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1.41 |
| 2 | 2.83 |
| 3 | 4.24 |
| 4 | 5.66 |
| 5 | 7.07 |
| 6 | 8.49 |
| 7 | 9.90 |
| 8 | 11.31 |
| 9 | 12.73 |
| 10 | 14.14 |

Because the shape never changes, a problem that gives you any one side has secretly given you all three. The two formulas sections below are just this ratio read in each direction.

## Formulas From the Leg

When a leg s is known:

| Quantity | Formula | For s = 6 |
|---|---|---|
| Other leg | s | 6 |
| Hypotenuse | s√2 | 8.49 |
| Area | s²/2 | 18 |
| Perimeter | s(2 + √2) | 20.49 |

The area formula is the ordinary (base × height)/2 with the two legs as base and height, which is why it collapses to s²/2. The perimeter is the two legs plus the hypotenuse, s + s + s√2, factored into s(2 + √2).

## Formulas From the Hypotenuse

When the hypotenuse c is known, run the ratio backwards:

| Quantity | Formula | For c = 14 |
|---|---|---|
| Each leg | c/√2 = c√2/2 | 9.90 |
| Area | c²/4 | 49 |
| Perimeter | c(√2 + 1) | 33.80 |

Two of these deserve a second look. The leg formula has two forms, c/√2 and c√2/2, which are the same number written differently; textbooks prefer the second because it keeps the square root out of the denominator. The area formula c²/4 looks unrelated to s²/2 but is identical underneath: substituting s = c/√2 into s²/2 gives c²/4. Check it against the table: a hypotenuse of 14 gives legs of 9.90, and 9.90²/2 ≈ 49, matching c²/4 = 196/4 = 49.

**Worked example (from the perimeter).** A garden bed shaped as an isosceles right triangle has a perimeter of about 34.14 meters. Find the sides.
Step 1: s = 34.14 / (2 + √2) ≈ 34.14 / 3.4142 ≈ 10.00.
Step 2: hypotenuse = 10√2 ≈ 14.14.
**Answer: legs of 10 m and 10 m, hypotenuse ≈ 14.14 m.**

## The Square Connection, Put to Work

Because this triangle is half a square, it appears anywhere squares get cut, measured, or checked along the diagonal.

**Tiles and sheets.** The diagonal of a square 12-inch floor tile is 12√2 ≈ 16.97 inches. The same multiplication gives the diagonal of plywood sheets, square rugs, and phone screens measured corner to corner, and it tells you whether a square object fits through a diagonal opening.

**Drafting and design.** The standard set square in a geometry kit, the transparent triangle used for drawing, is a 45-45-90 triangle. Its equal edges and exact 45° corners are what make it useful for drawing diagonals of squares and lines at 45°.

**Cutting material.** A square piece of fabric or paper folded or cut corner to corner produces two isosceles right triangles with no waste. Quilting and origami both build on this: the fold line is the hypotenuse, and its length is the side times √2.

## Can Its Sides Be Whole Numbers?

No, and this is a genuine theorem, not a gap in the list. If both legs were whole numbers, the hypotenuse would be leg × √2, and √2 is irrational, so the product can never be a whole number. An isosceles right triangle can have integer legs, like (5, 5, 5√2), or an integer hypotenuse with irrational legs, like (7.07, 7.07, 10), but never all three at once.

That is why this triangle never appears in the [Pythagorean triples](/blog/pythagorean-triples/) list, despite being the most symmetric right triangle of all. Triples need three integers; this shape can supply at most two.

## Common Mistakes

- **Making the hypotenuse one of the equal sides.** In a right triangle the equal pair is always the two legs. The hypotenuse is longer than either leg, so it cannot match one of them.
- **Assuming every isosceles triangle has 45° angles.** Only the isosceles *right* triangle does. A general isosceles triangle can have any vertex angle; the 45° base angles appear specifically because the vertex angle is 90° and the remaining 90° splits evenly.
- **Running the ratio the wrong way.** A leg is shorter than the hypotenuse. From the hypotenuse, divide by √2 (or multiply by about 0.707); from the leg, multiply by √2 (about 1.414). If your leg comes out longer than the hypotenuse, the operation went backwards.
- **Confusing it with the 30-60-90 triangle.** Both are special, but their ratios differ: 1 : 1 : √2 here, against 1 : √3 : 2 for the 30-60-90, whose legs are not equal. Check which angles the problem states before reaching for a ratio.
- **Forgetting the half in the area.** The area is s²/2, not s². The two legs are a base-height pair, and the triangle is half of the s by s square, so half its area too.
- **Rounding √2 too early.** Keep √2 symbolic through the calculation and round at the end. Using 1.4 from the start turns a hypotenuse of 14.14 into 14.0, an error that grows in any step that follows.

## Conclusion

The isosceles right triangle is the most predictable right triangle there is: two equal legs, angles of 90°, 45°, and 45°, and one ratio, 1 : 1 : √2, that converts any known side into all the others. Work from the leg with c = s√2 and area = s²/2, or from the hypotenuse with leg = c/√2 and area = c²/4, and remember that the equal sides are always the legs, never the hypotenuse.
