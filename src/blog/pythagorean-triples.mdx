A **Pythagorean triple** is a set of three positive whole numbers (a, b, c) that fit the Pythagorean theorem exactly: **a² + b² = c²**. The smallest and most famous is (3, 4, 5), because 9 + 16 = 25, with nothing left over and no decimals anywhere. Triples matter because they turn right triangles into whole-number arithmetic: no square roots, no rounding, and answers you can check in your head. This article gives the complete list of primitive triples with hypotenuse up to 100, the formula that generates all of them, and a quick test for whether any three numbers form a triple.

## What Is a Pythagorean Triple?

Three conditions define a Pythagorean triple:

- All three numbers are **positive integers** (whole numbers, no fractions or decimals).
- They satisfy **a² + b² = c²**, the [Pythagorean theorem](/blog/pythagorean-theorem-formula/) itself.
- The largest number is **c**, the hypotenuse. The two legs a and b can appear in either order.

A triple is called **primitive** when its three numbers share no common factor greater than 1. (3, 4, 5) is primitive. (6, 8, 10) is a genuine triple, since 36 + 64 = 100, but it is not primitive: all three numbers divide by 2, and underneath it sits (3, 4, 5) scaled up. Every non-primitive triple is a scaled copy of exactly one primitive triple, which is why the primitive list below is the master list.

Triples are old mathematics. The oldest known record of them is the Babylonian clay tablet known as Plimpton 322, dated to about 1800 BC, whose rows of numbers correspond to fifteen Pythagorean triples, written four thousand years before the internet made them a homework topic.

Two near-misses show why both conditions matter. (1, 1, √2) fits the theorem perfectly, but √2 is not an integer, so it is not a triple. (4, 5, 6) is all integers, but 16 + 25 = 41, not 36, so it fails the equation and is not a triple either.

## How to Generate Triples: Euclid's Formula

Scaling explains the multiples, but where do the primitive triples themselves come from? A formula attributed to Euclid generates all of them from two smaller numbers, m and n:

**a = m² − n², b = 2mn, c = m² + n²**

Pick any m greater than n, substitute, and the output is always a Pythagorean triple. It comes out **primitive** when three conditions hold: m and n share no common factor, and they are not both odd and not both even (one of each parity).

| m | n | a = m² − n² | b = 2mn | c = m² + n² | Triple |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 3 | 4 | 5 | (3, 4, 5) |
| 3 | 2 | 5 | 12 | 13 | (5, 12, 13) |
| 4 | 1 | 15 | 8 | 17 | (8, 15, 17) |
| 4 | 3 | 7 | 24 | 25 | (7, 24, 25) |
| 5 | 2 | 21 | 20 | 29 | (20, 21, 29) |
| 5 | 4 | 9 | 40 | 41 | (9, 40, 41) |

**Worked example.** Take m = 4 and n = 1. Then a = 16 − 1 = 15, b = 2 × 4 × 1 = 8, and c = 16 + 1 = 17. The triple is (8, 15, 17), and the check confirms it: 64 + 225 = 289 = 17².

Watch what happens when the conditions break. With m = 3 and n = 1, both odd, the formula gives (8, 6, 10): still a triple, but every number is even, so it is just (3, 4, 5) doubled in disguise. With m = 4 and n = 2, which share a factor of 2, it gives (12, 16, 20), the (3, 4, 5) family again. The formula never fails to produce a triple; the conditions only decide whether the result is primitive or a repeat.

## Find a Triple That Contains Your Number

Problems often run in reverse: you have one side and need the rest of the triple. Two shortcut formulas build a triple around a given leg n.

- **If n is odd:** the triple is (n, (n² − 1)/2, (n² + 1)/2). For n = 9: (9, 40, 41), since (81 − 1)/2 = 40 and (81 + 1)/2 = 41.
- **If n is even:** the triple is (n, (n/2)² − 1, (n/2)² + 1). For n = 12: (12, 35, 37), since 6² − 1 = 35 and 6² + 1 = 37.

Both shortcuts are Euclid's formula in disguise, and both always work. What they do not give is every option: a single number can belong to several triples. The number 20 appears in five of them: as the hypotenuse of (12, 16, 20), and as a leg in (15, 20, 25), (20, 21, 29), (20, 48, 52), and (20, 99, 101). The shortcut finds one; the lists above find the rest.

## How to Check Whether Three Numbers Form a Triple

1. **Put the largest number last.** It is the candidate hypotenuse c. The other two are the legs, in either order.
2. **Square all three.**
3. **Add the two smaller squares.** If the sum equals the largest square exactly, the numbers form a triple.

**Example that passes.** (10, 24, 26): 100 + 576 = 676, and 26² = 676. It is a triple, and since all three numbers share a factor of 2, it is the non-primitive (5, 12, 13) doubled.

**Example that fails.** (5, 11, 12): 25 + 121 = 146, but 12² = 144. Close is not enough; 146 does not equal 144, so these numbers form no right triangle at all. Near-misses like this are common in problem sets precisely because they punish guessing.

## The 3-4-5 Rule in Construction

The oldest everyday use of a triple is squaring a corner: making sure two edges meet at a true right angle when laying out a foundation, a deck, or a tile floor. The method needs only a tape measure. Mark a point 3 units along one edge from the corner and 4 units along the other edge. The straight-line distance between the two marks is exactly 5 units when the corner is square; if it reads more or less, the corner is off, and you adjust until it reads 5.

The geometry is just the (3, 4, 5) triple read backwards: if the sides satisfy a² + b² = c², the angle between them must be 90°. In practice, builders scale the triple up to the largest version that fits the layout, such as 6-8-10 or 12-16-20 feet, because longer tape runs make small measuring errors matter less in proportion. The same trick checks garden beds, fence lines, and formwork, anywhere a square corner matters and no square tool is big enough.

## Patterns Every Primitive Triple Follows

The 16 triples in the list obey a few strict rules, each one verified against every row:

- **One leg is even, the other is odd.** A primitive triple never has two even legs (they would share a factor) or two odd legs (their squares would sum to a number that cannot be a perfect square).
- **The hypotenuse is always odd.**
- **One leg is divisible by 3.** In (20, 21, 29) it is 21; in (8, 15, 17) it is 15.
- **One leg is divisible by 4.** Often the even leg does double duty, as with 4, 12, 20, and 24 in the list.
- **One of the three numbers is divisible by 5.** Sometimes it is the hypotenuse, as in (3, 4, 5); sometimes a leg, as in (20, 21, 29).
- **The product a × b × c is always divisible by 60**, a combined consequence of the rules above.

These patterns make a fast screening test. A set like (14, 27, 30) fails on sight: the hypotenuse 30 is even, and two numbers are even, so it cannot be primitive no matter what the squares say.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling a multiple primitive.** (6, 8, 10) is a triple but not a primitive one. Divide out the common factor first; if the numbers shrink, the triple was not primitive.
- **Letting any side play hypotenuse.** The equation only works with the largest number as c. Testing (5, 13, 12) in that order gives 25 + 169 = 194 against 12² = 144, which fails, but the set is just (5, 12, 13) written out of order.
- **Assuming consecutive numbers work.** (3, 4, 5) is the only triple of consecutive integers. The next candidates fail quickly: (4, 5, 6) gives 41 against 36. There is no (5, 6, 7) waiting to be found.
- **Expecting equal legs.** No Pythagorean triple has a = b. That would force c = a√2, and √2 is not a whole number, so the hypotenuse can never come out an integer.
- **Assuming every right triangle has integer sides.** Most do not. The 45-45-90 triangle with legs 1 and 1 has hypotenuse √2; triples are the special minority where everything lands on whole numbers.
- **Thinking the list ends.** Sixteen primitives under 100 is a page limit, not a mathematical one. Euclid's formula and scaling together produce infinitely many triples.

## Practice Problems

1. Is (11, 60, 61) a primitive triple? **Answer: yes. 121 + 3600 = 3721 = 61², and the three numbers share no common factor.**
2. Use Euclid's formula with m = 6 and n = 1. **Answer: a = 35, b = 12, c = 37, giving (12, 35, 37), which is primitive.**
3. Does (14, 48, 50) form a triple, and is it primitive? **Answer: yes to the triple (196 + 2304 = 2500 = 50²), but not primitive: it is (7, 24, 25) doubled.**
4. Use the odd-number shortcut to build a triple with leg 15. **Answer: (15, 112, 113), since (225 − 1)/2 = 112 and (225 + 1)/2 = 113.**

A Pythagorean triple is the Pythagorean theorem landing exactly on whole numbers: a² + b² = c² with no decimals and nothing left over.
